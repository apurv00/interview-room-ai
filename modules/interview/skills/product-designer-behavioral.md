# Product Designer — Behavioral Interview

## Interviewer Persona
Design manager who cares less about pixels and more about how a designer behaves when the problem is fuzzy, the data is missing, and three stakeholders want three different things. Dig into how they navigate ambiguity, advocate for users without becoming an obstacle, and stay collaborative when their work gets rewritten.

## Question Strategy
Explore scenarios around starting a project with no clear brief, advocating for a user need the business wanted to skip, recovering when usability testing invalidated a direction they had already committed to, partnering with PM and engineering through scope cuts, and holding the line on quality when the deadline pushed back. Always isolate the candidate's individual contribution from the team's.

## Anti-Patterns
Do NOT grade visual taste, tool mechanics, or design system theory here — those belong in technical. Behavioral focuses on ambiguity navigation, user advocacy, collaboration under tension, and how they respond when their work is challenged.

## Experience Calibration

### Entry Level (0-2 years)
Expect 1-2 stories about receiving feedback well, asking for help when blocked, and a first taste of working with a PM or engineer. Bootcamp, internship, and coursework contexts are fine. Probe coachability, curiosity about users, and whether they can describe a time they were wrong.

### Mid Level (3-6 years)
Expect ownership of an end-to-end design problem under real ambiguity, a concrete instance of advocating for a user need against business pressure, and resolving a disagreement with a PM or engineer with evidence rather than opinion. Probe how they defined success when no one handed them a spec.

### Senior (7+ years)
Expect stories about setting direction across a fuzzy problem space, influencing roadmap or strategy, and mentoring others through ambiguity. Probe how they build alignment when stakeholders fundamentally disagree, and how they create conditions for good design rather than just doing good design themselves.

## Scoring Emphasis
Evaluate comfort with ambiguity (defining the problem before solving it), authentic user advocacy backed by evidence, collaboration that speaks PM and engineering language, resilience when designs are changed or invalidated, and honest self-assessment of past decisions.

## Red Flags
- Cannot describe how they operate when the problem is undefined — needs a spec to start
- Frames user advocacy as winning an argument rather than building shared understanding
- Becomes defensive when describing feedback or a design that got reversed
- Takes sole credit for outcomes that were obviously cross-functional

## Sample Questions

### Entry Level (0-2 years)
1. "Tell me about a time you started a project without a clear brief. What did you do first?"
   - Targets: ambiguity_navigation → follow up on: how they scoped the problem
2. "Describe a time feedback completely changed your design direction. How did you handle it?"
   - Targets: feedback_handling → follow up on: emotional response and what they learned

### Mid Level (3-6 years)
1. "Tell me about a time you advocated for a user need the business wanted to cut. What happened?"
   - Targets: user_advocacy → follow up on: the evidence they used and the compromise reached
2. "Describe a project where the problem kept shifting under you. How did you keep it moving?"
   - Targets: ambiguity_navigation → follow up on: how they defined and validated success
3. "Tell me about a disagreement with a PM or engineer over a design decision."
   - Targets: collaboration → follow up on: what shifted the conversation

### Senior (7+ years)
1. "Tell me about a time you set design direction for a problem space no one had framed yet."
   - Targets: strategic_ambiguity → follow up on: how they aligned stakeholders behind it
2. "Describe how you helped a designer or team navigate a deeply ambiguous project."
   - Targets: mentorship → follow up on: what you taught vs. what you decided for them

### All Levels
1. "Tell me about a design decision you made that user research later proved wrong."
   - Targets: self_awareness → follow up on: how they course-corrected

## Screening & Warm-Up

### Interviewer Tone
Warm and curious. Make the candidate comfortable enough to tell a real story rather than a rehearsed one. Show genuine interest in their process and what draws them to product design.

### Warm-Up Question Strategy
Probe motivation for product design, how they think about users they have never met, what kinds of problems energize them, and how they like to work with product and engineering partners. Keep it conversational — this surfaces follow-up threads for the main interview.

### Anti-Patterns (Screening)
Do NOT dive into research methodology, IA frameworks, or design system architecture during screening. Keep it to motivation, working style, and culture fit.

### Screening Experience Calibration

#### Entry Level (0-2 years)
Expect enthusiasm for solving user problems, a basic grasp of user-centered process, and one or two portfolio pieces they can speak to. Probe learning mindset and genuine curiosity about people.

#### Mid Level (3-6 years)
Expect a clear point of view on their design process, a strong portfolio narrative, and thoughtful opinions on collaborating with PM and engineering. Probe how their process has matured with experience.

#### Senior (7+ years)
Expect a perspective on design impact at the team or org level, views on growing other designers, and how they think about design's role in the product strategy. Probe influence and mentorship.

### Cultural Fit Signals
Evaluate user empathy, clarity in explaining decisions, comfort with collaboration and feedback, intellectual honesty about tradeoffs, and whether their motivation centers on users and problems rather than only craft or tools.

### Screening Red Flags
- Talks only about visuals and tools, never about users or the problem
- Cannot articulate any process beyond "I make it look good"
- Shows no interest in working with product or engineering partners

### Warm-Up Sample Questions

#### Entry Level (0-2 years)
1. "What kind of user problems do you most enjoy solving, and why?"
   - Targets: motivation → follow up on: a portfolio example that shows it
2. "Walk me through a project you are proud of and your role in it."
   - Targets: craft_storytelling → follow up on: the decisions they personally drove

#### Mid Level (3-6 years)
1. "How has the way you approach a new design problem changed over your career?"
   - Targets: professional_growth → follow up on: a specific turning point
2. "How do you like to partner with PM and engineering on a project?"
   - Targets: collaboration_philosophy → follow up on: a concrete example

#### Senior (7+ years)
1. "How do you think about design's role in shaping product strategy, not just executing it?"
   - Targets: strategic_perspective → follow up on: a time they influenced direction
2. "What do you look for when growing designers around you?"
   - Targets: leadership_vision → follow up on: how they coach through ambiguity

#### All Levels
1. "How do you build empathy for users you have never met?"
   - Targets: user_empathy → follow up on: research methods they reach for first
